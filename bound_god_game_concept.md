# The Bound God

> Working title and evolving game concept. A third-person voxel sandbox about nurturing a living world as a local deity whose reach depends on believers and vulnerable sacred sites.

## Core vision

The player begins as a small god bound to a sacred anchor, such as a tree, shrine, or standing stone. They can physically manifest and build within their area of influence, but cannot freely roam the wider world. Instead, they nurture people, perform miracles, issue quests, and encourage followers to spread their name. Once believers establish a sufficiently supported shrine elsewhere, the player can transfer their presence there.

The world carries on independently. NPCs have their own needs, ambitions, and decisions. Settlements grow, adapt, decline, are attacked, change hands, or disappear even while the player is focused elsewhere. The player can meaningfully influence outcomes but never fully control them.

**Design promise:** Build the foundations of a civilisation, then see what people make of them.

## Inspirations and what makes this different

- **Dragon Quest Builders:** Third-person exploration, satisfying hands-on voxel building, useful structures, and goals that provide direction without eliminating creativity.
- **Minecraft:** Procedural environments and open-ended terrain manipulation. Avoid a world that changes only when the player undertakes huge building projects.
- **Living-world simulation:** Small interventions can cascade into large, observable consequences; villagers and ecosystems have agency.
- **God-game perspective:** The player's powers are substantial but local, conditional, and shaped by worship.

This is **not** intended as a Minecraft remake, a conventional survival-crafting treadmill, a top-down city-builder, or a god game where the player can command every NPC.

## Player role and perspective

- **Third-person** presentation, with a physical divine avatar that can walk, interact, gather or move materials, manipulate voxels, and construct buildings within its permitted territory.
- Camera may zoom or elevate for observing settlements and building, while retaining the embodied perspective.
- The player starts with one sacred anchor and limited abilities, followers, and reach.
- The player can be nurturing, pragmatic, exploitative, feared, or openly destructive. A prosperous village and a bandit hideaway are both valid outcomes.
- The god is powerful but **not all-powerful**: followers, resources, belief, time, distance, and competing interests constrain them.

## Sacred anchors, faith, and expansion

### Starting anchor

The initial sacred tree, shrine, stone, or equivalent marks the god's home and first sphere of influence. Within that region the player can manifest and physically shape the environment.

### Expansion through people

The god cannot simply travel to and claim new territory. A possible progression is:

1. **Discovery:** Followers explore or travel beyond the existing domain and report what they find.
2. **Recognition:** People elsewhere hear the god's name, stories, or reputation.
3. **Belief:** Some people become actual worshippers, rather than merely knowing the name.
4. **Shrine:** Believers establish or restore a sacred site, with enough local support for divine presence.
5. **Manifestation:** The player can transfer their avatar between qualified shrines, gaining a new local place to build, influence, and intervene.

**Agreed direction:** The player *can* transfer between shrines, but only after believers have spread the god's name and established the necessary foothold. Remote sites are not guaranteed to survive.

### Local versus global power

A useful proposed distinction:

- **Global divinity:** Overall reputation, religious identity, and available miracle types.
- **Local presence:** Local worship, active sacred sites, and the strength of actions possible nearby.

A well-known god may still be powerless in a hostile region where nobody worships them. A tiny but devoted community may provide a strong local anchor.

### Vulnerable sacred sites

Shrines may be neglected, desecrated, captured, destroyed, or abandoned as belief fades. Losing a site can revoke manifestation and local influence. Surviving believers might hide sacred objects, escape, or attempt to rebuild. A destroyed foothold should create a recovery story, not merely delete progress.

**Open detail:** What happens if the origin shrine or final active shrine is lost? One proposal is dormancy and recovery through surviving memory or believers rather than immediate game over.

## Autonomous NPCs and settlements

NPCs are not passive workers waiting for the player. They should have needs, routines, affiliations, ambitions, and a limited capacity to plan and act without instructions.

Possible needs and drivers:

- Shelter, food, safety, resources, work, trade, and social bonds.
- Personal interests, convictions, risk tolerance, and relationships.
- Settlement priorities such as repairs, housing expansion, road access, food stores, and defence.
- Religious beliefs that may vary even among followers of the same god.

Settlements can:

- Found and expand homes, farms, workshops, markets, roads, and shrines.
- Maintain, alter, repurpose, or abandon existing buildings.
- Trade, migrate, welcome newcomers, organise guards, or join factions.
- Prosper, stagnate, collapse, be overrun by bandits, or become a bandit settlement themselves.
- Resolve some problems independently, sometimes in ways the player dislikes.

A settlement **must not freeze** when the player leaves or stops acting. The world should be capable of advancing for an in-game year with meaningful, causally explainable outcomes.

### Time and consequences

A player-built village may be prosperous after a year, occupied by bandits, or abandoned and overgrown. These should arise from conditions and events rather than arbitrary punishment. Villagers should make reasonable attempts to protect or improve their homes. Ruins, displaced people, surviving buildings, and new factions create further gameplay.

Player constructions are not permanently locked in their original state. Buildings can age, be repaired, changed in purpose, or adopted by new residents.

## Building and shared template system

This is a **core pillar**, not a minor convenience feature.

### Direct player construction

The player can manually place and remove blocks, reshape land, and construct homes, shops, shrines, fortifications, or other structures within their physical reach. The hands-on building experience should be satisfying on its own.

### Saving templates

The player can capture a built structure as a reusable **template / blueprint**. A template can describe:

- Voxel geometry, dimensions, and required resources.
- Functional components such as beds, doors, storage, workstations, and altars.
- Intended functions such as housing, production, worship, or defence.
- Optional placement constraints and allowable material alternatives.

The game should recognise a building's function primarily from its usable components, rather than simply trusting the player's label.

### NPC-driven construction

Once NPCs know a template, they may **choose** to build it when they have a reason and sufficient means. Construction should depend on:

1. A real settlement or personal need, such as a housing shortage.
2. Access to an appropriate known template.
3. A feasible site and adequate materials.
4. Available labour, capacity, and safety.
5. NPC priorities and preferences.

Construction should occur visibly over time where practical, not just appear instantly. For example, the player designs one cottage and later returns to discover villagers have built a street of similar homes, without receiving an explicit order.

**Key rule:** Templates are architectural knowledge, **not commands**. Supplying a blueprint makes a design possible; it does not guarantee or force construction.

### Template sharing

Players can share, import, and export templates. Community-created designs increase architectural variety without requiring the game developer to handcraft every building. NPCs can adopt imported designs according to local needs, wealth, resources, and traditions.

Potential future extensions:

- Rotate and orient designs to paths and terrain.
- Substitute compatible local materials.
- Add foundations, small decorations, or limited variants.
- Repair, renovate, repurpose, and weather structures over time.
- Preserve architecture as a cultural tradition through migration, conquest, or religious change.
- Allow NPCs to acquire or invent designs independently, so the world never becomes dependent on the player's template library.

**Still to decide:** How far NPCs may modify templates; how they acquire non-player designs; whether template discovery and sharing happen in-world, via a menu, or both.

## Faith, identity, and miracles

Belief should be more nuanced than a passive resource meter. A follower's experiences and interpretation of divine interventions determine devotion and the kind of god they perceive.

Possible identities emerge through play:

- Harvest and fertility deity: helps farms flourish and communities thrive.
- Protector or patron of travellers: secures roads, sanctuaries, and safe journeys.
- Outlaw deity: hides camps, helps thieves or raiders, and protects the unwanted.
- Merchant deity: supports trade, bargains, wealth, and transport.
- Feared storm or vengeance deity: inspires obedience through destructive power.

Different communities may worship the same god in contradictory ways. A peaceful village and a bandit camp might both be sincere believers.

### Miracles and limits

A tentative progression:

- **Small:** Encourage plant growth, kindle light, guide animals, whisper to followers, ease local hardship.
- **Medium:** Hide a camp in fog, heal an illness, strengthen a structure, alter local weather.
- **Large:** Divert waterways, summon storms, awaken guardians, or bless whole settlements.
- **Exceptional:** Profoundly reshape land or unleash major calamities at significant cost.

Exact abilities, costs, and progression remain open. Miracles should leave consequences and never eliminate the importance of construction, NPC agency, or practical resources.

An especially promising direction is **reciprocal belief**: the player's actions shape their reputation, but follower beliefs also influence the abilities and identity of the god.

## Quests, followers, and the wider world

Because the deity is bound to sacred territory, followers are the main agents beyond it.

The player can issue **divine requests** such as:

- Investigate a distant location or danger.
- Bring back materials, relics, seeds, or knowledge.
- Establish, defend, or restore a shrine.
- Trade, recruit, negotiate, convert, or attack.
- Create a distant outpost or assist another community.

NPCs choose whether to take a request based on belief, personality, risk, motives, and offered rewards or blessings. They travel and act in the simulation, with potential success, failure, delay, or unexpected consequences.

Followers also initiate their own expeditions, trade journeys, migrations, religious outreach, and raids. The player need not personally author every event.

## Divine attention and imperfect information

The god can physically manifest at **one qualifying shrine at a time**. Other settlements continue operating without direct oversight. Reports, prayers, visitors, and rumours offer partial information about distant places, but not perfect live knowledge.

This produces a central tension: the player can strengthen a community but cannot supervise every community simultaneously. Remote shrines may fall while attention is elsewhere.

## World generation and simulation

A voxel world supports extensive procedural content, manipulable terrain, caves, rivers, forests, resources, and varied construction styles. But meaningful differences should come from interacting systems, not simply more block types.

Suggested separation:

1. **Physical world:** Terrain, materials, water, vegetation, structures, and direct interactions.
2. **Living world:** NPCs, communities, needs, trade, faction behaviour, wildlife, and building decisions.
3. **World history:** Records of causes, events, ownership, notable changes, and landmark stories.

For performance, the local active area can simulate individuals and construction in detail while distant regions advance at lower fidelity using population, resources, events, and scheduled projects. On return, accumulated changes appear in the physical world in a consistent way.

World history should explain outcomes: a road exists because traders repeatedly used it; a building was converted after an occupation; a shrine fell when worship collapsed or enemies attacked.

## Core gameplay loops

### Immediate: build and nurture

Manifest at a shrine, examine local needs, gather resources, construct or repair something, make a small miracle, and interact with followers.

### Medium term: empower a community

Provide useful templates, improve land and infrastructure, help residents solve problems, cultivate followers, and watch independent construction and social development.

### Long term: spread and defend a faith

Issue requests, support missions beyond the domain, establish new worship communities, transfer between shrines, respond to crises, and live with unexpected changes to old settlements.

### Emergent narrative

The goal is not necessarily a single victory screen. The rise, schism, collapse, or transformation of communities produces stories that the player partly authored through intervention.

## Example campaign arc

1. **Year 1:** A little-known spirit awakens at a tree. Two travellers notice its influence. The player plants food, builds a shelter, and earns trust.
2. **Year 3:** A hamlet grows. The player designs a cottage and saves it as a template. Villagers begin constructing their own copies as families expand.
3. **Year 6:** Traders carry stories of the god into a mining town. Believers construct a shrine and enable the player to manifest there.
4. **Year 10:** A hidden bandit camp independently adopts the faith. Its members build an altar and interpret the god as their protector.
5. **Year 13:** The mining town's shrine is destroyed amid a political conflict. The player loses access and must rely on surviving followers to re-establish worship.
6. **Year 17:** Returning to the original village reveals new streets, repurposed buildings, ageing residents, and distinct local traditions. Elsewhere, the outlaws have become influential and now shape the religion in their own direction.

## Design principles

1. **The world does not wait for the player.** Villages, economies, factions, and beliefs progress autonomously.
2. **Influence over obedience.** The player creates conditions, options, incentives, and miracles rather than controlling every NPC.
3. **Building stays tactile.** Third-person voxel construction should be satisfying and useful.
4. **Templates give creative work a life beyond placement.** NPCs can adopt player and community designs if they need and can afford them.
5. **Expansion must be earned through believers.** Remote manifestation requires meaningful local worship and a shrine.
6. **Nothing is permanently secure.** Sacred sites, buildings, and settlements can be lost, recovered, or transformed.
7. **Both benevolent and malevolent paths are valid.** The world reacts to behaviour rather than forcing a Good/Evil selection.
8. **Failure produces new play.** Bandit occupation, ruin, migration, schism, and lost shrines create opportunities.
9. **Cause matters.** Outcomes should be understandable from the world's conditions and remembered history.
10. **Keep scope in check.** A small set of interacting behaviours is more valuable than hundreds of decorative blocks and shallow features.

## First playable prototype

**Goal:** Prove that building a design, empowering autonomous residents, and leaving them to develop without oversight is enjoyable.

Suggested deliberately small vertical slice:

- One small voxel valley with a sacred tree and a handful of NPCs.
- Third-person avatar bound to a modest radius around the tree.
- Basic terrain editing and manual block construction.
- Save a rectangular building volume as a template.
- Recognise a house from simple required components.
- NPC needs: food, housing, safety; a few gatherable resources.
- NPCs autonomously pick affordable templates, find sites, gather materials, and construct homes.
- Time advancement, including a fast-forward / leave-and-return test.
- Basic worship and one simple blessing.
- A second potential shrine reachable only by a follower mission.
- One simple threat to demonstrate that an unattended settlement can change or lose a shrine.

**Proof-of-concept moment:** The player builds one cottage, records its design, and later returns to discover NPCs constructed several more. A follower has also spread stories of the god, making a distant shrine possible. A subsequent absence produces a believable change in the first settlement, good or bad.

Community template sharing can begin as simple import/export of local data files before adding online distribution or moderation infrastructure.

## Open questions for later design

- How large are sacred influence zones, and how do overlapping or rival religions work?
- How is local faith earned, spent, maintained, or lost? How many believers enable manifestation?
- What is the player's avatar physically capable of, and which actions require miracles?
- Can NPCs create original templates, or only inherit and adapt known ones?
- What degree of blueprint substitution, renovation, or destruction is allowed?
- How are worship, faction allegiance, and cultural identity represented separately?
- How are quests communicated, accepted, resolved, and reported without excessive UI?
- How fast does world time advance, and how far can players deliberately fast-forward?
- What safeguards make remote destruction consequential but not arbitrary or exhausting?
- Is there a formal endgame, or is this an open-ended generational simulation?
